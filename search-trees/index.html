Momentus · research log

What six thousand LLM-written strategies taught us about LLM-driven search

Between June and September 2026 a tree search looked for intraday trading strategies for the Nifty 50, using sixteen language models to write its candidates. The search picked which strategy to refine. A model wrote the refinement as code. The backtester scored it, and that score decided what the search picked next. It did this a few thousand times. This log is about what it found, what it was really optimising, and what its scores do and do not mean.

Chapter 1

Catalan

Catalan is a research programme about intelligent search. It does not ask a system to solve a stated problem. Its framing is the one the programme states: here is a space of possibilities. Learn what is worth exploring. Several properties can make a search intelligent in that sense. One is that the policy deciding what to try next is updated by what earlier attempts returned. Another is that the thing being searched for is an algorithm rather than a setting of parameters. These two are the ones this log can speak to.

The programme puts six questions and holds them open rather than assuming the answers.

  1. Adaptive search. Whether a system can change its search policy while it is still solving the problem.
  2. Compute allocation. How to distribute a fixed budget across competing hypotheses.
  3. Pruning. Whether reasoning can rule out unpromising regions before paying to evaluate them.
  4. Algorithm discovery. Whether search can discover algorithms at all.
  5. Transfer. Whether search knowledge carries across unrelated problems.
  6. Scaling. How performance changes with reasoning, evaluation and search compute.

The programme, its six questions and an index of every finding it has published are kept in one public repository: github.com/Amit-Prasad/catalan-research. This log is one of those findings.

Chapter 2

The question

Intraday trading is the domain of this case study. It is unforgiving in the right ways. The data is public and plentiful. Ground truth arrives whether or not you want it. Costs are real and known. The signal is thin enough that a search which merely looks clever gets caught.

The case bears on the second, third and fourth of the programme's questions, and on the explore-exploit balance that runs through all six. Above all it is a search at low signal-to-noise ratio (low SNR). Any real edge a strategy has is small next to the noise in its backtest, so a score can rank luck as readily as skill. It says nothing about transfer or scaling, because this is one domain on one budget. Trading is not the subject of the log. What the search was actually doing is.

The short answer is that the search did discover algorithms. It then spent most of its budget discovering a defect in the scorer. That is a finding about compute allocation under a noisy objective. It is why chapters 7 and 8 are about the measurement rather than the strategies.

Chapter 3

What we built

  • Pathfinder is a Monte Carlo tree search over programs, built for algorithm discovery. The tree decides where the next backtest goes. A language model is then asked to write the refinement that goes there, and the result is fed back into the rule that makes the next decision. That rule is PUCT-style and chapter 6 sets it out. Each node is a complete strategy as code and a child is a refinement of its parent. The whole thing is therefore one long explore-exploit decision. Every expansion costs a backtest and the budget is finite. Each unit of it goes either into refining a strategy that already scores or into opening a branch barely tried. PUCT makes that call per expansion, weighing what a node has scored against how little it has been visited. The rule has two modes, global and per-node. Both searches ran mostly in the global one and the second later switched to the per-node one. Each snapshot in the explorer, which is the Tree tab above, records which was in force and the exploration constant it used. Chapter 6 sets the rule out. The approach follows ERA (Aygün et al., arXiv:2509.06503). Pathfinder is our own implementation and the credit for the method belongs to that work.
  • A data pipeline holds minute-by-minute price bars for the Nifty 50, the fifty large companies in the NSE's headline index, from January 2023 onward. The bars are stored locally, so a backtest costs compute rather than API quota. Every strategy in this log was backtested on those fifty names and no others.
  • A backtester runs intraday, long and short, and applies Indian transaction charges and slippage per trade.
Chapter 4

Definitions

Every term this log depends on is fixed here and used in one sense throughout. The first list below names the parts of a search. The second names what a backtest reports. Chapter 5 states each measurement as a formula and says what it was doing in the score, and a cross-reference after a definition points to the formula.

The search

Search
One run of Pathfinder, from a starting strategy to the last one it tried.
Node
One strategy: its program, its backtest metrics and its score. A count of nodes is a count of strategies tried.
Parent, child, branch, depth
A child is a node the model produced by refining its parent. A path down the tree is therefore a lineage of successive tweaks to one idea. A branch is one such lineage from the starting strategy. Depth counts the refinements along it. A top-level branch is one first tweak to the starting strategy, and depth 68 is a chain of 68 refinements.
Snapshot
A saved copy of an entire tree at one moment.
Evaluation score
The single number the search ranked a node on, computed from the metrics its backtests returned. The selection rule that the search uses is based on this score. see (6.1)
Visits
How many evaluations happened at a node or anywhere below it. Expanding a node credits a visit to it and to every one of its ancestors, so a node high in the tree accumulates its whole subtree's count. The root's visits equal the number of strategies tried. Dot size shows it in the explorer, which makes it the record of where the budget went. see (6.1)

The measurements

Backtest
A replay of a strategy over a stretch of past minute bars, such as June 2024. Its rules run bar by bar as if it were trading live, and every trade pays real costs. The result is how the strategy would have performed had it been running during that stretch. see (5.1)
Window
One slice of past time a backtest runs over. Its length is a setting and any span can be used. The two the search drew on most were a 30-day stretch, for example 1 to 30 June 2024, and a range of anywhere from 15 to 30 days. A strategy is never judged on one window alone. It is backtested on several windows, and its score combines the results.
Sharpe ratio
Return divided by its own volatility: reward per unit of jumpiness. see (5.2)
Drawdown
The worst fall from a high point along the way. A drawdown of 20 per cent means the account stood a fifth below the highest value it had reached in that window. see (5.3)
Number of trades
How many round trips a strategy took across all its windows. see (5.4)
Profit factor
Gross profit divided by gross loss. A value above one implies the winners outweigh the losers. see (5.5)
Profit percentage
What a strategy returned over its windows, after costs. see (5.6)
Charges and slippage
The fees and taxes on a trade, plus the gap between the price the strategy assumed and the price it actually filled at. Both are charged inside the backtest. see (5.7)
Look-ahead bias
Use of information that did not exist yet at the moment a decision is taken. A rule may use only what had already happened by that time. An example is a rule that buys at 10:00 on the strength of that day's closing price.
Chapter 5

What the score was made of

The search ranked every strategy on a single number. That number was built from a handful of measurements of a backtest. Which measurements they were, and how they combined, is the whole of what the search was optimising. This chapter takes them before any history: what each one says, why it was in the score, and what it misses alone. Chapter 7 gives the formulas that combine them. see (7.5)

Each measurement is computed on one window first. A backtest records the value of the account once per minute bar, and every measurement below is a statistic of that series or of the trades that produced it. Chapter 7 then says how the per-window numbers were pooled into a window set and how the sets combined.

(5.1) Backtest: the equity curve and its per-bar returns, with E0 the capital at the start of the window
Et = E0 + realised PnLt + unrealised PnLt  ·  rt = EtEt−1 − 1
(5.2) Sharpe ratio: the mean of the window’s bar returns r divided by their standard deviation, then scaled up to a year
Sharpe = mean of rstandard deviation of r × √A
where A is the number of bars in a trading year, which is 252 days × 375 minutes a day ÷ m minutes a bar, so A = 94,500 on one-minute bars
Sharpe = 0 if the standard deviation of r is 0 or the curve has fewer than three points
(5.3) Drawdown: the worst fall below the running peak as a percentage of it, where peakt = maxs ≤ t Es
Drawdown = maxt peakt − Etpeakt × 100
(5.4) Number of trades: one trade is one closed round trip, and a window set adds up the windows in it
Tradeswindow = count of closed round trips  ·  Tradesset = Σi Tradesi
(5.5) Profit factor: taken over the net profit or loss pi of each trade in the window
Profit factor = Σpi > 0 pi| Σpi < 0 pi |  ·  ∞ if the denominator is 0 and the numerator is positive  ·  0 if both are 0
(5.6) Profit percentage: the only measurement the score never used
Profit % = Efinal − E0E0 × 100
(5.7) Charges and slippage: taken on every trade inside the backtest, with b the slippage in basis points. This is what makes pi in (5.5) a net number
pi = (exit − entry) × quantity − charges  ·  entry = price × (1 + b / 10⁴)  ·  exit = price × (1 − b / 10⁴)
charges = brokerage, capped per order + exchange fee + regulator fees + tax on those + securities transaction tax on the sell + stamp duty on the buy

One matter of scale is worth stating before chapter 7 uses these. Drawdown is a percentage, and the score divides by one plus it. A strategy that drew down 20 per cent therefore has its score divided by 21 rather than by 1.2, while a strategy that barely traded keeps a denominator near one. The term is far stronger than its form suggests.

One property of the combination matters more than any single term: the score multiplies rather than adds. A Sharpe near zero collapses the whole product, however good the profit factor. A profit factor in the hundreds lifts everything, however bad the Sharpe. Chapter 8 is largely about what the search did with that.

Chapter 6

How the search chose what to try next

A tree of a thousand strategies offers a thousand places to spend the next backtest. The rule that picks one is as much the search as the model that writes the code. That rule is a variant of PUCT, the selection rule from AlphaZero.

It scores every candidate as a sum of two terms and takes the largest. The first of those terms is exploitation. In the per-node mode the candidates are one node's children, and the term is each child's rank among its siblings by best score so far, normalised to run from 0 for the worst to 1 for the best:

Exploitation, per-node mode: a node with k children sorts them by best score so far, so rank(child) is 0 for the worst and k − 1 for the best
exploitation(child) = rank(child)k − 1
Exploitation, global mode: the same, taken across all N nodes in the tree at once
exploitation(node) = rank(node)N − 1

With three children, for example, the worst scores 0, the middle one 0.5 and the best 1, however far apart their scores are. In the global mode the candidates are every node in the tree at once, and the rank is taken across all of them. Chapter 7 says which of the two modes each search ran. Every snapshot carries the mode it ran under, which the explorer shows as MCTS for the per-node mode and PUCT for the global one.

The second term is exploration. It is the constant 1.4 times the square root of the parent's visit count logged over one plus the child's own visits. A node nobody has tried has a small denominator, so its exploration term is large. Every visit shrinks it. That is the whole of the balance. A branch keeps being refined until its untried siblings become the better bet by arithmetic.

(6.1) Per-node mode, which a snapshot records as MCTS: the rule runs from the root downwards, child against sibling, until it reaches a leaf. At each step the candidates are one node’s k children, ranked by best score so far, with rank 0 for the worst and k − 1 for the best, and c = 1.4
U(child) = rank(child) / (k − 1) + c × √( ln visitsparent / (1 + visitschild) )
(6.2) Global mode, which a snapshot records as PUCT: the candidates are all N nodes at once, ranked together, and the exploration term carries a constant factor of 1 / N
U(node) = rank(node) / (N − 1) + c × (1 / N) × √(Σ visits) / (1 + visitsnode)

One property of this implementation matters for everything that follows, and it is not obvious from the name.

Exploitation runs on rank, not on score. Siblings are sorted and spread evenly between 0 and 1. A node that leads by a factor of five is treated exactly like a node that leads by a whisker. The search cannot tell a large edge from a small one. It knows only the order. That matters for chapter 8. A corrupted score need not be wildly wrong to misdirect the budget. It need only put the wrong node on top.

Chapter 7

What the search did, and how it was scored

Work spans 21 June to 27 September 2026, and it was not one search but two. The first ran from 21 June to 12 July and the second from 15 August to 27 September, with a five-week gap between them. Neither main search ran continuously; each ran in bursts. The logs record about 6,200 expansion attempts, from which 3,109 unique strategy versions survive across 20 distinct snapshots. A snapshot is a saved copy of a whole tree at one moment, as chapter 4 defines it. Several snapshots hold the same strategies re-scored under a different rule. Every one of them opens in the explorer, and the links through this chapter draw the one being discussed in the panel beside the text. Sixteen models took part, several of them under more than one label.

The scoring rule changed several times while the searches ran. This chapter follows events in order, and writes out each rule at the point it came into force, so every score below can be read against the formula that produced it. The measurements inside the formulas are the ones defined in chapter 5. Every score is an evaluation score: the number the search ranked on, not a return.

Node numbers belong to a search, not to a strategy. The second search began a fresh numbering, so 403 node ids exist in both searches. In every one of those cases the two nodes are different programs. Every node number quoted in this log is from the second search.

First search · 21 June – 12 July

How a strategy was scored at the start. Each candidate was backtested on two sets of windows. One set was fixed for the whole run (val₁). The other was drawn fresh for each strategy (val₂). Each set mixed four kinds of window, and their scores were combined with fixed weights:

(7.1) One set of windows, June – July 2026: 30-day windows, 15–30-day ranges, single days and single hours
S = 0.4 × S30-day + 0.3 × Srange + 0.2 × Sdays + 0.1 × Shours
(7.2) The two sets blended into the strategy's score
S = 0.4 × Sval1 + 0.6 × Sval2
(7.3) The score on each kind of window, 21 – 28 June: a weighted sum, with no trading costs modelled
Score = wret · Returngross + wsh · Sharpe − wdd · Drawdown

Because the terms are added, they act independently: a good Sharpe can make up for a bad drawdown. By the evening of 21 June the search had tried 56 strategies. By 27 June it had tried 68.

28–29 June: costs and a trade bonus. Two things entered the score: transaction charges of 0.03% of turnover, deducted inside the backtest, and a fixed bonus for every trade a strategy made, up to a target number of trades.

(7.4) The new score, from 28–29 June
Score = wret · Returnnet + wsh · Sharpe − wdd · Drawdown + C · min(total trades, target trades)
where Returnnet is the return after charges, C is the bonus paid per trade, and the bonus stops growing once a strategy reaches the target number of trades

All 68 strategies were re-scored. The explorer keeps that tree on 27, 28 and 29 June, as the change went in. Over those three days its best strategy scored 2.25, then 14.67, then 1.65, and the share scoring above zero fell from 93% on 28 June to 7% on 29 June. Same strategies, different rulers.

Growth and pruning, to 9 July. After that the tree grew fast: by 7 July it held 1,270 strategies. It was then pruned, and by 9 July it held 970. On 9 July a bug in the backtester's trailing stop was also fixed.

Early July: a multiplicative score. The sum in (7.3) was replaced by a product, and the new rule was applied retroactively to every strategy then in the tree. This is the rule every later score in this log comes from, so it is given in full:

(7.5) The score for one set of windows, from early July 2026
Score = Sharpe × log2(1 + max(trades/yr, 0)) × Profit factor1 + Drawdown
where
trades/yr = total tradesyears span   years span = simulated days365.25
boundaries
total trades = 0 ⟹ Score = −5.0  ·  years span unset or 0 ⟹ 1.0  ·  profit factor ∈ {∞, NaN, none} ⟹ 5.0
(7.6) Inside a set of N windows, the measurements are pooled before scoring. Profit factor is averaged as a mean of ratios, which chapter 8 returns to
Profit factorset = 1N Σ Profit factori    Sharpeset = 1N Σ Sharpei    Drawdownset = 1N Σ Drawdowni    Tradesset = Σ Tradesi

Because the terms multiply, any factor near zero collapses the score, and any factor that grows large multiplies everything else. The sign of the score is the sign of the Sharpe ratio: the trade term and the profit factor are never negative and 1 + Drawdown is always positive, so a strategy with a negative Sharpe always scores below zero. Six of the snapshots in the explorer are the same 970 strategies scored six different ways while this rule was being settled, rather than six moments in time. Under three of the variants one strategy scored −5.8, −6.2 or −7.7.

11–12 July: merged branches. From 11 July a strategy could have more than one parent. In branch merging the model is shown two or more strategies that scored well and asked to combine them. It might take one strategy's entry filter and another's exit logic. The result inherits from several lineages instead of refining one. In a 254-node snapshot from 12 July, 33 strategies were built that way, adding 94 extra parent links to the 253 ordinary ones. Such a snapshot is a graph rather than a tree. The explorer and the panel beside the text place each strategy under one parent and draw the extra links dashed. Those strategies are also not independent samples of the search, because their lineages overlap. The first search ended on 12 July.

Second search · 15 August – 27 September

A restart, and a three-stage validation. The nine strategies judged usable from the first search were re-seeded into a fresh tree, numbered from scratch. Every candidate now passed through up to three stages. The diagram shows their shape, and two of the failures in chapter 8 are visible in it before they are argued in prose.

Each candidate was backtested on two sets of randomly chosen windows. One set was fixed for the whole run (val₁). The other was drawn fresh for that strategy and did not overlap the first (val₂). Single days and single hours were dropped, so each set now held two kinds of window, and each kind was scored with (7.5) and the pooling in (7.6).

(7.7) One set of windows, August – September 2026: six 30-day windows, plus 15–30-day ranges totalling 180 days
S = 0.6 × S30-day + 0.4 × Srange  ·  years span in (7.5) = 180365.25 ≈ 0.4928 for either kind
(7.8) Stages 1 and 2 blended. The factor of 0.5 is why the stage-3 trigger below reads 1.0 rather than 2.0
S1,2 = 0.5 × (0.4 × Sval1 + 0.6 × Sval2)
(7.9) Stage 3, on ten more windows from 1 Jan 2023 to 1 Aug 2026, fixed for the run, plus a look-ahead audit in which two models read the code for future-peeking
Sval3 = 0.6 × Sval3, 30-day + 0.4 × Sval3, range
(7.10) The strategy's final score, as the tree ranked it

Two details of the blend across sets are not averages. The blended drawdown is the maximum of the two sets. In this path an infinite or missing profit factor is replaced by 3.0 rather than the 5.0 in (7.5).

A strategy then went to dry-run deployment only if all four of these held:

  • final tree score ≥ 0.25
  • Sval3 ≥ 3.0
  • a static code check passes, rejecting forward indexing such as shift(-1)
  • the two-model look-ahead audit passes

Growth, merging and trimming. A change of selection rule sent the tree wide, up to 268 children under one strategy. Branch merging, as in the first search, was used here too: a 17 August snapshot has 12 strategies carrying 24 extra parent links. The tree reached a peak of 1,501 strategies on 14 September. It was trimmed to 119 the same day, then regrown and merged into the final 603, whose longest branch was 68 refinements long.

What the numbers said. By its own scores the search was working. The best strategy of the second search reached 32.5. In its peak tree 798 of 1,501 strategies produced code that ran, and 123 of those scored above zero. Four strategies cleared the gates and went to a dry run. No real money was ever committed.

Every number in this chapter and every score in the explorer is an evaluation score, computed on windows drawn from the same January 2023 to August 2026 period the search was free to explore. The rule changed three times, so a score is comparable only with others from the same snapshot. None of them is a return, and none was measured on data the search had never seen.

Chapter 1 said this search spent most of its budget discovering a defect in the scorer. Chapter 8 is the evidence: it shows where the rules above were wrong.

Chapter 8

What was wrong with it

Chapter 7 ends on numbers that look like success: a best score of 32.5, 123 of 798 evaluated nodes above zero, four strategies through the gates and into a dry run. This chapter is why none of that is evidence of anything about trading.

The re-reading happened because those numbers were too good for a problem this hard, so the scoring code and all twenty snapshots were gone through line by line. It turned up nine problems. Each is a failure of a rule chapter 7 states, and the first two are visible in that chapter's diagram. Together they are what chapter 1 meant by a search that spent its budget discovering a defect in the scorer. Findings 1 and 2 void the leaderboard outright. The rest shaped what the search chased instead.

  1. There was no unseen data

    Every window at all three stages came from January 2023 to August 2026, the period the search explored freely. A strategy could be refined until it fit that period. Nothing in the loop could distinguish fit from discovery. see (7.9)

  2. The last line of defence fed the search

    A fraction of the third-stage score was added to the evaluation score. Part of the third-stage results was shown to the model when it proposed the next refinement. The check became another surface to fit. see (7.10) now (9.3)

  3. Some third-stage results belong to a different strategy

    Four nodes in the second search's final tree carry an identical third-stage payload on four different programs. The score, the profit, the drawdown and the trade count agree to six decimals, while the parents and the first-stage metrics differ. see (7.9)

    Different code cannot produce identical results on identical windows. The windows were generated once per run and shared by every candidate, so each result file was named only after its dates. When a run failed to produce its file, the reader fell back to the newest file in the directory, whichever strategy had written it. Node 439, holder of one of the highest third-stage scores in the tree, shares its number with node 597. At least one of those two was gated on another strategy's backtest.

  4. The score paid for rarity, not for trading

    In the second search's peak tree, not one of the 340 evaluated strategies that made 100 trades or more scored positive. Median profit falls monotonically with activity. Nineteen of the top twenty made 25 trades or fewer across all their windows, which is roughly twelve months of data. see (7.5)

  5. One window's profit factor set the top of the leaderboard

    The backtester aggregates profit factor correctly, by pooling gross profit and gross loss. The search layer above it averaged the per-window ratios instead, mapping infinities to a constant but leaving large finite values untouched. A single window with a near-zero loss then dominates the average, and the score is linear and unbounded in it. A cap of 5.0 on each window's ratio would have prevented this. The surrounding code caps nothing but an actual infinity. The scores in the explorer are the uncapped ones, which is how a set of windows reports a profit factor of 85.6 at all. see (7.6) now (9.1)

  6. Sharpe was computed on minute bars

    Annualising a Sharpe ratio from minute returns inflates it. It inflates most for strategies that hold briefly and rarely, which are the strategies the trade term already favoured. Daily returns are the only honest basis. see (5.2) now (9.2)

  7. The audit was bypassable, and was bypassed

    Two of the four dry-run deployments went out with the look-ahead audit or the third-stage gate overridden by hand. A gate that can be waived under time pressure is a gate that reports on discipline, not on code. see the gates in §7

  8. Fills were optimistic

    Entries and exits were taken at prices the following bar would not necessarily have offered. A bar that touches both the stop and the target had no rule deciding which came first. Both errors favour the strategy. see (5.7)

  9. There was no luck baseline

    Nothing in the loop ever asked how many winners random entries with the same exits and the same costs would have produced. Without that number, 123 positive nodes out of 798 is not a result.

Chapter 9

What the new validation shows

In progress. The rebuilt evaluation runs four tests, and the results will be added here as dated updates rather than replacing this chapter:

· every working strategy re-scored with corrected fills · a locked holdout period no search run has touched · a random-entry baseline with the same exits and costs, to price luck · a second, later holdout kept for the finalists alone.

Three rule changes govern them, each aimed at one of the failures in chapter 8:

(9.1) Profit factor: pooled as a ratio of sums, and capped, which is the rule finding 5 asks for
Profit factorpooled = min( Σ gross profitiΣ |gross lossi| , 5.0 )
(9.2) Sharpe ratio: from daily returns rather than minute bars, so the annualisation factor is 252 rather than 94,500
Sharpedaily = mean of daily returns − risk-free ratestandard deviation of daily returns × √252
(9.3) The search objective: with the third stage removed from it and from the model's feedback
Search score = 0.4 × Sval1 + 0.6 × Sval2

Whatever those tests say is the ending of this log. A result that kills the strategies is still a result about the search.

Chapter 10

Lessons for search

Chapter 8 was about this search's own measurement. These are the parts that survive being lifted out of it. They apply to any intelligent search over a noisy objective, which is what chapter 1 says the programme asks about.

  • A search finds the scorer's bugs before it finds anything else. The single most successful discovery this search made was that one near-zero-loss window makes an unbounded profit-factor term enormous. It then spent 41% of the final tree refining that discovery. see (8.5)
  • Under noise, greedy selection converges on luck. Rare traders won because rarity is the cheapest way to buy variance. Any scorer that does not price the number of observations will rank sampling error. see (8.4)
  • A bonus term that is not monotone in what you want is an invitation. The log₂ trade term was meant to reward activity. It was weak enough that avoiding activity remained the better move, so the search did that instead. see (7.5)
  • Reuse is a silent correlation between nodes. Results keyed to their inputs rather than to the program they came from will be shared sooner or later. A shared evaluation looks exactly like agreement between independent strategies. see (8.3)
  • In an automated loop, reliability beats capability. Two models produced runnable code from nine attempts in ten. Three produced almost none, and their budget bought nothing.
  • When the idea channel fails quietly, the search keeps moving and stops thinking. The fallback prompt used when a model's idea could not be parsed produced 60% of the last snapshot's nodes and 11 of its top 20, including the best node in the tree. The logs still look like reasoning.
  • Depth is not progress. The tree went from 268 top-level branches at depth 5 to 10 branches at depth 68, with 70% of nodes under three of them. Failures rose and positives collapsed.
Chapter 11

What is next

Three things, in order. First, correct the fills so entries and exits use prices a bar actually offered. Second, lock a holdout period and remove the third stage from the score and from the model's feedback. Third, re-run every working strategy under those rules, against the holdout and against a random-entry baseline. Then this log gets its chapter 9.

Pathfinder itself is the second thread. It needs one command to run and a README that credits ERA properly. It then needs a benchmark against random sampling and plain evolutionary search at equal model budget, on one ERA task and one problem with an exact score. If those results hold up they belong in a preprint.
